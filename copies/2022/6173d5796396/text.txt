 Inside the great Pyongyang crypto caper

 When a group of guileless blockchain entrepreneurs found themselves invited to North Korea, it seemed like a weird adventure – until the travellers found themselves wanted by the FBI, accused of helping a rogue state evade international sanctions. Was it really a criminal conspiracy, or just a case of crypto bros gone wrong?
 By Peter Guest 

 24 November 2022 

 Julia Dufossé 

 The invite to North Korea arrived via LinkedIn. It was the summer of 2018 when Chris Emms, a British blockchain entrepreneur, received a message from the Korean Friendship Association. A volunteer network of supporters of North Korea, the KFA is charged with boosting North Korea's public image in the West and around the world. Its head and founder, a Spanish activist by the name of Alejandro Cao de Benós de Les y Pérez, was arranging a cryptocurrency conference in Pyongyang, he explained. Would Emms like to come?

 The invite was not as incongruous as it might sound. Emms, then in his mid-20s, had spent most of his short career as a nomad on the international blockchain circuit. Back then, crypto was a travelling circus, with dozens of countries vying to capture a piece of an industry on the rise, and Emms had gone with it, moving from job to job and country to country, living itinerantly in the crypto world’s shifting encampments: Andorra, Dubai, Malta, Gibraltar, accepting the invitations to speak where they came. In crypto terms he was a booster, more than he was a builder (that is: he doesn’t actually code), drawn in by the unchained utopianism that characterised the movement’s early days.

 “Crypto was such a different world back then, it really was. You know, it felt like you could do anything, the possibilities of changing the world were endless,” Emms said.

 Even in crypto , a world full of eccentrics, the man behind the invite stood out. A former IT consultant, Cao de Benós once owned a short-lived North Korean theme bar in Tarragona, Spain, and is typically pictured in a military uniform bedecked with medals. At the KFA, Cao de Benós works to promote the North Korean political philosophy of juche – an offshoot of Marxism which emphasises self-reliance and “autonomy from great powers.” The inaugural Pyongyang Blockchain and Cryptocurrency Conference would assemble interesting thinkers from the blockchain and crypto space, while providing a unique opportunity to visit North Korea. Guests would be charged $5,000 (£4,500), including travel, to attend.
 Emms didn’t need much convincing. The invitation appealed to his idealism and his curiosity. At the time, the news was full of talk of a detente between the US and North Korea. Then-US president Donald Trump had met Kim Jong-un in Singapore, promising an end to decades of isolation and tension. Former basketball star and self-appointed US goodwill ambassador to North Korea Dennis Rodman arrived at the summit wearing a MAGA baseball cap and a T-shirt with the logo of Potcoin, a marijuana-backed cryptocurrency that had sponsored Rodman’s peace-building tour of Pyongyang. “The news was flooded with, you know, Donald Trump hugging Kim Jong-un,” Emms said. “So honestly, [I] was thinking, you know, this is a really cool opportunity to build bridges and make peace… it felt like an opportunity to see somewhere that was about to change radically.”

 In hindsight, it’s tempting to look for a philosophical intersection of the two groups — the juche true believers preaching resistance to imperialism, and the crypto-idealists, with their doctrine of freeing finance from the all-seeing eye of state control.

 But Emms never put that much thought into it. He could not have anticipated that three years later, one of his fellow attendees would be serving a five-year prison sentence in New York, that Cao de Benós would be wanted by the FBI, or that Emms himself would be on bail in Saudi Arabia, accused by the US government of conspiring to aid North Korea launder billions to fund its nuclear weapons programme. He just figured it was a chance to see somewhere new and different, and spend a couple of days talking crypto.
 “I thought it would be a quirky thing to do,” he said.

 Julia Dufossé 
 When Emms landed at Pyongyang Sunan International Airport in April 2019, he was struck by how new it was, and how empty. The airport was rebuilt at great expense in 2012, but only a handful of flights land each day from Russia and China, banks of check-in desks and luggage carousels sit idle.
 There were eight conference-goers: the youngest in their early twenties, the oldest in his mid-forties. After months of Telegram and WhatsApp messages, the delegates had met in Beijing for the transit to Pyongyang on a commercial flight full of tourists and North Koreans returning from shopping trips to China. Upon arrival, they were bundled into a screening area, to have their electronic devices examined. Emms had been warned in advance that this might happen. One attendee from Argentina had tried to smuggle in a sex tape hidden among his files, just to see if he could. The North Korean inspectors found it in short order, and the Argentinian attendee had his laptop taken off him. The group was driven to their hotel, where they were chastised for embarrassing their hosts. “The guides told us… how ashamed they were, and how embarrassed they were and how this is reflected badly on them,” Emms said. “Our passports were taken and confiscated at that point.”

 Before the pandemic, a few thousand Western tourists visited North Korea every year, drawn by its isolation from the homogenising effects of global capitalism. Visitors to Pyongyang are presented with scenes of scripted normality and modernity – airports, hotels , restaurants and supermarkets that exist for foreigners and TV cameras to show the nation’s prosperity and progress, all of which have an unsettling two-dimensionality, like theme park sets.

 Over the past 20 years, the country has also become a kind of punchline for a younger generation whose view of the country has been filtered through social media and American pop culture. The current leader, Kim Jong-un, and his father and predecessor Kim Jong-il, have become memeable despots – comical and cartoonish in their revolutionary Mao suits and state-mandated haircuts. For a certain kind of extreme online personality – including the overlapping fraternities of hackers, crypto bros and shitposters – a trip to Pyongyang has taken on a surreal appeal.
 “It’s one of the coolest shitty places that you really want to explore,” Fabio Pietrosanti, an Italian technology entrepreneur and digital rights activist, who attended the Pyongyang forum, told me over a late-night Zoom call this summer.

 For Pietrosanti, getting access to North Korea was something between a running joke and a puzzle. He recalled a game that he and some fellow activists played during a drinking session at a digital rights conference in 2018, in which the winner was the first among them who could make a phone ring in Pyongyang. He later spent months trying to figure out whether it would be possible to employ North Koreans to make a piece of urology software that he’d been idly thinking about for a few years after a health scare. He even met the North Korean ambassador to Italy to try to set up the scheme. (The ambassador defected to South Korea a few months later.) Going to a crypto conference in Pyongyang and using the conference centre wifi to hack into the national intranet, just to poke around – as Pietrosanti claims he and another attendee did – was, in the internal logic of this community, a natural next step.
 The people who turned up for the conference were hardly a collection of crypto royalty. As well as Emms and Pietrosanti, there were a couple of crypto investors, a cybersecurity specialist, and the writer Ethan Lou (who declined to be interviewed for this piece). The notable exception was Virgil Griffith, an American hacker once profiled in The New York Times Magazine as the “Internet Man of Mystery.” Griffith, at that time living in Singapore, was one of the architects of the Ethereum cryptocurrency and, as a law enforcement professional who later investigated the conference said, “the only high profile person there.”
 Visitors to Pyongyang are presented with scenes of scripted normality and modernity – airports, hotels, restaurants and supermarkets that exist for foreigners and TV cameras to show the nation’s prosperity and progress

 For the first five days, the trip had little to do with crypto. They were picked up in the mornings and taken on tours of local businesses, accompanied at all times by their handlers. The facilities seemed modern and well-run, but somehow thin – unconvincing facsimiles of real companies. At a solar panel factory, Pietrosanti asked if the owners wanted to export to Italy , and was told that, no, local demand was too high to think about exporting the products, as the country moved to a juche -compliant, self-sufficient grid. (Only 30 per cent of North Koreans have access to stable electricity, according to US government statistics. Most of the country’s power comes from hydroelectric power and coal.)
 At a foreign language school, they were told that teachers were using augmented reality to educate students, but the only hardware on display was a PC running Windows 10, a seven-year-old piece of software. They were given a chance to interact with selected teachers and students – supervised at all times. “What we learned was that they mostly had a good understanding of technical and commercial terms,” Pietrosanti said, “but they had never seen a picture of a British [double-decker] bus. They’d never seen Big Ben or the Statue of Liberty… the regime trained them to communicate in English as a language, but not as a culture.”

 It was clear that they were only seeing an illusion of Pyongyang. As they were shuttled from place to place in their official minibus they could see how the illusion was maintained: every few hundred metres, work gangs sat on the roadside, pulling up weeds by hand.
 They spent most evenings in the hotel bar , a small, dingy room in which they were usually the only drinkers. It was there, according to Pietrosanti, that they had their only overt brush with illegality, when a hotel guest who introduced himself as a Taiwanese businessman asked if they’d be interested in brokering deals for oil transfers with Russia through bank letters of credit – a way to hide the money trail behind sanctions-defying deals for fuel. They demurred.
 Whenever they left the bar, they would sometimes find one of their guides lurking behind a pillar, waiting for them.
 When the conference finally took place, the event was a farce. For two days, the group was cloistered in Pyongyang’s Sci-Tech Complex, a conference centre, science museum and library housed in a building shaped like an atom. In the public areas were computers running bootleg copies of Windows XP, an operating system discontinued in 2008. The speakers were given a few printed sheets of approved material, which had been agreed in advance with the local organisers by Cao de Benós. It was mostly very basic and a little incoherent – pages taken from Google, a commercial brochure and a couple of academic papers, barely enough to fill an hour’s worth of seminars. “Calling it a conference is a bit of an overstatement,” Emms said.

 In another situation it might have been funny, but their handlers were sure to remind them of the grave embarrassment caused by the sex tape incident. They hinted darkly that there could be consequences if the event didn’t go well. “Bear in mind no one had their passports,” Emms told me.

 As the conference attendees began to run out of material, a degree of panic set in. Their North Korean hosts were getting hostile. No one had their return flight tickets yet either, or any way of getting to the airport. To keep the conversations going, they took it in turns to ad lib, riffing on the material they were allowed to present, repeating themselves. “Imagine if you have random cards on the table and you need to play a role game: which shitty brochure are you going to talk about?” Pietrosanti said. He ended up using a two-page flyer to speak about “real estate tokenisation in blockchain,” a subject he knew absolutely nothing about. The sole motivation, he said, became: “we need to get back home safely, so let’s make them happy.” Griffith gave the keynote, if you could call it that, wearing a Mao suit, on the topic of “blockchain and peace.”
 Basic as the material was, it seemed to be going over the heads of the handful of locals who attended, who were mainly middle-aged functionaries. Some of them slept through the sessions. Only a few thousand people in North Korea have access to the internet, mostly people deep within the security apparatus, or university students in very specialised disciplines. More have access to a very limited national intranet, but in total only a tiny fraction of the population have the skills and access to make use of this kind of technical information.

 All week, Pietrosanti had been nagging the group’s guides to take them for pizza – he’d heard about an Italian chef who’d set up in the city, principally to serve diplomats. But the Italian embassy had advised staff to leave, and what was left “was not Italian,” Pietrosanti said. They ate “shitty pasta and shitty pizza,” and enjoyed a few bottles of Emilia-Romagna red wine left behind by the previous owners.
 On the last day, there was one last fright waiting for Emms. As their minibus waited outside the hotel to take them to the airport, he was summoned back inside and escorted back to his room, where three women who looked like maids were lined up outside, alongside the three “guides” who had accompanied his group all week. The authorities had “suspicions” about him, they said, and were searching his room. They didn’t elaborate. Emms denied everything, unsure what it was he was denying. Get back on the bus, they said – and don’t tell anyone else about this.
 When Emms landed back at Gatwick, he discovered that the trouble might have followed him. At passport control, he was taken aside by someone who had identified himself as a police officer. He wasn’t in any trouble, the man said, but had he attended a conference?
 As their minibus waited outside the hotel to take them to the airport, he was summoned back inside and escorted back to his room… The authorities had “suspicions” about him

 Panicked, when he reached his flat in London, Emms googled the security services tip line, thinking, he said, that “maybe there’s something here that, you know, needs to be cleared up… that I wasn’t trying to hide anything or cover anything up or anything like that.” The line was answered by “some woman, who didn’t really seem to care at all. She said, ‘I’ll take your details. If we’re interested, we’ll call you back.’ That’s as far as it went.”
 No one ever called him back. Two months after the crypto event, Donald Trump met Kim Jong-un in the DMZ (the Korean Demilitarized Zone, a strip of land bordering North and South Korea), becoming the first sitting US president to cross into North Korea. Kim praised the two men’s friendship; Trump hinted that sanctions could be relaxed. In a Telegram group set up among the crypto forum’s attendees, Cao de Benós started talking about arranging another event. This time, he suggested, they should go skiing . “You know, at that point, no one really thought that what we’ve done has been particularly wrong,” Emms said. He toyed with the idea of another trip. “I thought it might be interesting to go skiing in North Korea, but I don’t think I would have gone,” he told me. “I don’t think there was anything else left to see. I think my curiosity had been satisfied.”

 By that autumn, the North Korea Telegram groups had mostly gone quiet, but Emms, now working in business development for the crypto trading platform bitcoin.com, had kept in intermittent touch with Griffith with a view to setting up some kind of partnership. Emms moved to Malta, an emerging crypto hotspot.
 Late one evening that November, he got a message from a friend in the US, with a link to breaking news. Griffith, back in the US for Thanksgiving , had been arrested under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, a piece of legislation designed to enforce American sanctions on North Korea. Griffith was charged with conspiring to help Pyongyang use cryptocurrencies to launder money and evade sanctions. Presented as evidence was a photo of Griffith in a dingy room in the Pyongyang Sci-Tech complex, in his Mao suit, in front of a whiteboard — in the corner of which was written “no sanctions yay =).”

 By Aaron Toumazou 

 “It was an earth-shattering moment,” Emms said.
 In hindsight, it’s easy to see why US security services became interested in the Pyongyang forum. By late 2019 it had become clear that the North Korean government’s interest in cryptocurrency extended far beyond an amateurish conference.
 Under strict economic sanctions since the mid-2000s, the Kim regime has often used crime to generate hard currency. Cheap North Korean methamphetamine (crystal meth) is a blight across Asia, while the country became so good at forging US currency that its counterfeit $100 was known as the “super dollar.” Over the last decade, the regime has also bet big on cybercrime, recruiting promising engineers from the Pyongyang University of Automation into “Bureau 121,” the cyberwarfare division of military intelligence.
 It was most likely a unit of Bureau 121, known in the cybersecurity world as the Lazarus Group, that hacked Sony Pictures in 2014. Lazarus had previously gone after political targets in South Korea, but, as sanctions on North Korea tightened in response to nuclear tests during the 2000s and early 2010s, the group shifted wholesale to theft. It robbed banks in Vietnam and Ecuador in 2015. Then, in 2016, it conducted an audacious raid on the Bangladesh Bank, using the Swift banking network to try to transfer $1 billion (£875 million) from the South Asian nation’s central bank’s account at the US Federal Reserve. A basic spelling error in the transfer instructions alerted the Fed, which blocked most of the transactions, but the attackers succeeded in moving more than $100 million (£92 million) into accounts in the Philippines and Sri Lanka. More than $60 million (£55 million) has still not been recovered.

 NFTs: everything you need to know

 By Duncan Cooper and Jessica Klein 

 The stolen funds are used to support the country’s dictatorship, which is accused of human rights abuses on a massive scale , including internment camps for political opponents, long-term conscription, arbitrary detention and execution and almost complete restrictions on free speech and free movement. Hundreds of thousands of people are believed to be starving in the country, which continues to invest millions – possibly billions – of dollars a year into developing an illegal nuclear arsenal. Although it is hard to comprehensively fix a timeline, experts agree that around 2018, North Korea’s hacking groups began to focus much more intently on crypto. Bitcoin’s rise in value from around $1,000 in early 2017 to close to $10,000 in 2018 helped drive a global boom in crypto exchanges, which were moving hundreds of millions of dollars in largely untraceable currency through infrastructure that had few of the security features of the formal banking system. “This was an emerging industry with a lot of capital, and basically no security,” Aaron Arnold, an associate fellow at the Royal United Services Institute think-tank, told me. “So it was sort of like an open door that North Korea just had to walk through.”
 According to the United Nations Security Council, North Korean hackers made off with at least $890 million (£820 million) in crypto between January 2017 and November 2020 – an estimate that could well be conservative, since many hacks go unreported or unattributed. In 2021, they stole a further $395 million (£365 million), according to Chainalysis, a security firm that investigates crypto-related cybercrime. In a single heist last March, they stole $615 million (£570 million) in wallets from players of Axie Infinity, a game in which players compete to earn non-fungible tokens (NFTs) .

 The crypto industry has invested heavily in supervision and surveillance to try to make it harder to launder the proceeds of cybercrime. To evade detection, criminals such as the Lazarus Group rely on a thriving underbelly of opaque “mixers” that turn one form of cryptocurrency into another, and “over-the-counter” brokers in Russia and China who will do off-the-books cash deals for large volumes of crypto. They extract a high price, but the North Koreans don’t care if they only make 50 cents on the dollar.

 “It’s pure profit,” said Nick Carlsen, a former FBI investigator who now works as an intelligence activist for TRM Labs, a consultancy that investigates crypto crime. “You have a team of like, 15-20 guys doing this, and they pull in as much money as the labour of maybe 100,000 coal miners and shipping guys and gold traders. I mean, it’s just incomparable value.”
 According to the United Nations Security Council, North Korean hackers made off with at least $890 million (£820 million) in crypto between January 2017 and November 2020 – an estimate that could well be conservative

 The North Koreans are particularly dangerous, Carlsen said, because unlike other cybercriminals they have no fear of the authorities. Other criminals may eschew mega-heists because of the heat they would face from police, or because they may have to spend years carefully laundering the proceeds. The North Koreans, who are already confined to their country and sponsored by the state, just steal as much as they can and cash out as quickly as they can, Carlsen said. “The killer is that they don’t care about getting caught.”
 This has been enormously frustrating for the US government, which has made cracking down on sanctions breaking and the darker reaches of crypto a strategic priority. In February 2021, the US charged three North Koreans, Jon Chang Hyok, Kim Il and Park Jin Hyok, for participating in crypto thefts. The previous year, two Chinese brokers, Tian Yinyin and Li Jiadong, were charged with laundering the proceeds of crypto heists. All five remain at large.
 There have been a few successes. In 2020, a Nigerian social media influencer, Ramon Olorunwa Abbas, who went by the name of “Hushpuppi,” was extradited from the UAE facing a wide range of charges, including helping launder money from a suspected North Korean crypto heist. In September 2021, a dual US-Canadian citizen, Ghaleb Alaumary, was sentenced to 11 years in jail after pleading guilty to helping launder money stolen from a Maltese bank by North Korean hackers. This year, the US government sanctioned two crypto mixers, Blender.io and Tornado, which it said have been used by the North Koreans to launder the proceeds of their theft.
 Then there is Griffith. His arrest is a polarising topic in the community of North Korea watchers and cybersecurity experts. On the one hand, there is not much sympathy for his decision to go to the forum. As an American, it was illegal for him to go without State Department clearance. (He was denied permission but went anyway.) There was that “no sanctions, yay” picture. And, experts are quick to stress, North Korea’s regime has brutalised and starved its people for decades . It is not a punchline.
 But on the other hand, the charges against Griffith felt overblown. According to the DoJ’s indictment against him, Griffith had apparently told investigators that the North Koreans had come away from the forum with a better understanding of blockchain – an admission that “ technology transfer” had taken place. People who know Griffith told me that they saw this as bravado, and evidence of his naivety – he had his first meeting with the FBI without a lawyer, and was apparently unaware of the risk he was taking. Independent experts who have examined the material presented at the conference said that there was nothing shown there that the North Koreans couldn’t have found online. And an investigation by the United Nations body charged with monitoring sanctions violations concluded the same.

 Emms declined to comment on his interactions with Griffith, but Pietrosanti, and others who investigated the event, told me they think Griffith attended mainly as a stunt – he liked to troll the media and thumb his nose at authority. “Because Virgil is personally an arsehole,” Pietrosanti said. “I mean, I love him, but he’s an arsehole, he’s a very provocative person.” According to Pietrosanti, the material Griffith presented was, like the rest, just taken off the internet.
 “I think it’s a bit silly to think that Virgil Griffith somehow provided North Korea with some sort of sanctions evasion knowledge they didn’t already have,” said Arnold, who was the finance and economics expert on the UN Panel of Experts for DPRK sanctions in 2019, and led its investigation into the Pyongyang blockchain event. “I think that North Korea was well aware of what crypto was and how to use it.”
 In September 2021, a dual US-Canadian citizen was sentenced to 11 years in jail after pleading guilty to helping launder money stolen from a Maltese bank by North Korean hackers

 One explanation is that the whole event was simply a scam, aimed at extracting a little money out of the attendees, and boosting the profile of the KFA and its founder, Cao de Benós. The North Korean agencies who interact with overseas groups like the KFA or with would-be do-gooders offering development assistance to the country are good at extracting small amounts of money, opportunities for foreign travel, or computer equipment from visitors. “Historically, the North Koreans have been pretty good at finding ways to get something in terms of, you know, money or materials, through these kinds of interactions,” Sokeel Park, director of research and strategy at Liberty in North Korea, an international NGO, told me.

 For the KFA, getting a bunch of foreign nationals to come to Pyongyang and meet local businesses helped to justify the organisation’s existence.
 In 2020, Cao de Benós was the subject of a Danish documentary, The Mole , directed by Mads Brügger. The film portrays the Spaniard as a would-be criminal, working to broker deals for North Korean-produced drugs and weapons in Europe and Africa. Cao de Benós has denied the allegations against him. Several experienced North Korea analysts, including former intelligence agents and people formerly actively involved in investigating sanctions violations, told GQ that they suspected exaggerated claims of drug smuggling and arms trafficking had actually helped the KFA, making the organisation seem far more significant and effective than it actually is. In reality, they said, it is a small and often amateurish group of “fanatics and true believers,” surviving on small grants from North Korean embassies and under pressure to prove it offers value for money.

 Certainly, some of the Pyongyang conference attendees felt that they were the product, offered up by Cao de Benós, who they suspect pocketed part of their fees. “The only villain of the story is Alejandro,” Pietrosanti said. “It was a scam.”
 Cao de Benós did not respond to multiple requests for an interview, but sent a statement saying: “I was not present in Korea or during the event, so I cannot give you specific details of what was said between the adults present there.” He added: “The charges from the USA are ridiculous and have no evidence.”
 He remains in Spain, which typically doesn’t extradite its citizens for offences that aren’t considered crimes in Spain. However, Dermot Hudson, the chairman of the UK branch of the Korean Friendship Association, agreed to meet at Charing Cross station in London . We sat together to talk on a Westminster park bench. Hudson, a former trade unionist and civil servant, talked at length about what he said were the international “misconceptions” about North Korea. According to Hudson, North Korea’s people are not starving, there are no widespread human rights abuses and summary executions, and the country’s nuclear weapons programme is a sign of its economic vitality.

 He claimed that defectors to South Korea were brainwashed into criticising the north, and were often criminals fleeing justice. (There is no evidence to support this or any of his other claims.) After Griffith’s arrest, Emms said he contacted US authorities via a lawyer, hoping to head off any legal problems.  “It felt for a while, it felt like, you know, finally I could get on with my life again,” he said. “But obviously, that wasn’t the case.”

 Emms sat in the police station in the middle of the airport, smoking , with the officers guarding him. He’d given up cigarettes, “but that seemed like a time when I needed them,” he said. No one was able to explain to him what was happening. Even his guards seemed confused. “They were sort of saying to me: ‘Oh, America, blacklist, America’,” he recalled.
 He was sent to a cramped detention cell with 20 other men, mostly Indians and Egyptians waiting to be deported. From there he was taken, shackled, to an interrogation centre and put in front of a local prosecutor, who told him he was being held on an Interpol red notice – an international arrest warrant – that had been issued by the FBI. He had two choices, the prosecutor said: accept deportation and be flown to the US, or stay in Saudi while the local authorities weighed up the case.
 The DoJ’s indictment of Emms closely resembles that of Griffith. It argues that Emms and Cao de Benós “recruited” Griffith. It alleges that after Emms was stopped by police in London, he messaged other attendees urging them to delete photos from the event. (Emms declined to comment on the specific allegations.)
 Six months later, Emms is still in Saudi Arabia. His accounts, including his crypto wallets, have been frozen. Because he’s not a Saudi resident, he can’t get an apartment, so he’s moving from hotel to hotel, using whatever deals he can find online and paying with his mother’s credit cards.
 His guards seemed confused. “They were sort of saying to me: ‘Oh, America, blacklist, America’,” he recalled

 He doesn’t know anyone in the country, and the only person he gets to see is one of his lawyers, who occasionally visits for coffee and to update him on his case. “It’s like being in solitary confinement,” he told me over a Zoom call. The connection kept dropping out due to the weak hotel wifi. “It’s hard to describe what it does to you mentally. I’ve definitely had some dark, dark days.”
 In the months since his arrest, he became obsessed with googling the prison conditions in New York, where he anticipates ending up in pre-trial detention if the extradition order goes through. He talked about prison stabbings, COVID outbreaks, Jeffrey Epstein’s suicide. “You’re in there with everything from murderers, rapists, all the way down to white collar criminals, which is just crazy. It’s an incredibly dangerous place to be,” he said. “If we fight this out in court, in the US, I’d be looking at spending a very long time in that prison.”

 Radha Stirling, an activist who has campaigned against abuses of Interpol’s red notice system, and has been supporting Emms’s case, suspects that the US authorities chose to trigger the warrant when Emms was outside the UK to sever him from his support networks and wear him down, so that he accepts extradition.
 Issuing a red notice and fighting to extradite a suspect is not a trivial process. It means expending political capital to lobby countries to push for extradition. And it sets uncomfortable precedents. “If Saudi Arabia tried to extradite a US citizen from Britain for violating Saudi sanctions, we’d be outraged,” Stirling said.
 It is unlikely that Cao de Benós will be extradited from Spain, which is typically resistant to sending its citizens overseas. “If that happened,” Hudson, the KFA chairman said, “we would organise a campaign in support of Alejandro. He would be the KFA’s Julian Assange.”
